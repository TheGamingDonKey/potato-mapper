# 02 — Horror cinematography, composition and lighting

Research date: 2026-10-15. Specialist remit: an original Halloween projection direction dominated by **dark enchanted forest and spectral creatures**. This is a research and treatment document, not implemented media or product code.

## Judgment

Make the forest itself the threatening presence. Establish a place with foreground occluders, a readable opening and a distant light; let a large creature become recognizable inside that established space. A spectator should first wonder whether a tree moved, then understand that something much larger than a person has been standing there. That gives the collection an ambition beyond small characters floating on black.

The desired emotions are awe, fascination and dread. The sources below support particular filmmaking practices; they do **not** demonstrate that these proposed animations will be scary or mesmerizing on Shane's projector. No motion reference was watched in this research pass, no still image was visually inspected, and no animation, performance benchmark or physical projection was produced. Source inspection was webpage text, including firsthand interviews and archival accounts. Video embeds and image captions are not evidence of watching footage.

## Practices translated into decisions

These are **original design inferences** informed by the accounts above. They are proposed decisions for the new collection, not claims that the cited filmmakers used these exact forest effects.

| Practice | Concrete decision for this projection collection | Failure to avoid |
|---|---|---|
| Negative space | Give the viewer a specific empty route to inspect: a gap between two trees or a strip of pale water. A small moving light on one side implies the opening should contain something. Change that opening only after it has become familiar. S5 motivates this approach. | A mostly blank image with random decoration does not establish an expectation. An empty area needs surrounding geography and a reason to watch it. |
| Silhouette | Draw the creature through interruption of a brighter, already visible background. Use a distinctive head-to-shoulder relationship and unequal limbs; reveal only enough contour to identify a scale and a pose. | Tiny bright eyes, rounded head and bouncing motion can read as friendly icons. A full glowing outline explains the whole creature immediately. |
| Motivated light | Establish one principal source, such as a veiled moon or an ember caught in a root. Its direction determines rim highlights, branch shadows and reflected water. A supernatural violation happens once, after that rule is clear. S1/S3/S4 motivate the hierarchy. | Every object glowing independently destroys the viewer's ability to infer a shared world. |
| Occlusion | Let a foreground trunk repeatedly cover different parts of one large form. Keep its apparent anatomy consistent as it passes through gaps. The reveal is an interruption of the forest's existing light, not an arbitrary opacity fade. | A head, hand and body that appear in unrelated locations read as unrelated effects. |
| Reveal | Progress from environmental consequence, to partial contour, to a short held recognition. The consequential movement comes after recognition: an antler turns while its shadow refuses to turn. | An immediate full creature entrance has no period of uncertainty; endless withholding has no payoff. |
| Depth | Use three clearly different planes: near trunks with sharper silhouettes, middle-distance roots or water, and one distant source. Keep their occlusion order stable. Small differential movement can imply depth without a sweeping camera. | Uniform haze lifts the whole frame, competes with the creature and adds a visible projected rectangle. If mist is used, give it a bounded depth layer and a story function. |
| Restraint | Give each 24-second loop one governing impossible event and one main focal transfer. Quiet does not mean frozen: a branch settles, ripples travel, or fine dust crosses a beam. S2/S5 inform the controlled framing. | Continuous flashes, particles, screaming faces and simultaneous motions prevent a meaningful before/after. |

## Domestic projection changes the cinematography

**Black means minimum added projector light, not a patch of physical darkness painted onto a wall.** A useful approximate model is `observed wall light = ambient/reflected room light + projector black leakage + projected image contribution`. Surface reflectance and geometry affect each term. A black source pixel cannot subtract the porch light falling on white siding. This is a physical translation, consistent with S6's ambient-light limitation, not a measured model of this installation.

Consequently, a black silhouette succeeds when it interrupts a sufficiently visible *projected* background. It cannot reliably hide an already bright physical window frame. Design the forest around the actual target: use existing dark gaps for missing creature detail and place backlit clearings on surfaces that can carry them. Keep any necessary simulated shadow within the scene's own illuminated region. The idea is to alter how the architecture is perceived, not assume the architecture disappears.

Also distinguish source compositing from projection optics. Under ordinary Add and Screen compositing, a black foreground does not darken a brighter image underneath. A creature silhouette must therefore be precomposed with its clearing into the same loop, or use an actual supported masking/occlusion route verified in the app. Do not stack a glowing forest and a separate black creature and expect subtraction. This compositing implication follows the supplied Screen/Add constraint; those exact code paths were not independently audited by this specialist.

Use the app's existing image and looping-video surfaces as the first delivery route. Rich offline lighting, texture, deformation and layered depth can be rendered into a single loop; they do not require a runtime volumetric renderer. C++17/Qt6/OpenGL 3.3 and AMD 840M are supplied constraints, not a performance result. File format, decode load, native projection resolution, synchronization across surfaces and loop seeking still require real playback verification. A single precomposed surface is the simplest artistic prototype; a carefully matched multi-surface version can follow if the installation needs it.

## Shared art and exposure rules for the three treatments

All numbers below are **proposed artistic targets**. `Y` means relative linear image luminance in the graded master, with reference white `Y=1`; it is not an sRGB code value, projector power percentage, nit reading or proven visibility threshold. The final encode must use the correct transfer function. The hue names describe direction, not a demand for oversaturated RGB colors.

- Give each scene an environment spanning the usable frame, with a creature eventually occupying about 45–75% of frame height. This proposed scale directly addresses the rejected miniature-mascot direction.
- Reserve `Y=0` for true source-negative areas; shape most readable environment around `Y=0.04–0.18`, the main reveal around `Y=0.25–0.45`, and small source accents up to `Y=0.65`. Avoid large white fields. These ranges may need substantial raising or simplification after a wall test.
- A face need not have luminous eyes. Recognition can arrive from muzzle, beak, joint angle, interrupted background and a small reflective edge. Creature surfaces should have broad, readable forms before fine detail.
- Keep viewpoint stable so the projected surface feels like an opening. Any virtual camera move should be slow and small; the apparent creature can move through depth while the frame stays locked.
- Build a cyclic base environment whose geometry, light and secondary motion return at 24 seconds. Hide the creature's return behind an opaque trunk or a source-negative interval. Matching just the first and final still frame is insufficient: velocity, lighting and particle phase must match too.

## Treatment A — The Forest That Holds Its Breath

**Emotional ambition:** an immense spectral stag has been mistaken for part of the forest. Awe comes from scale, dread from one part of it behaving independently of the rest. This is an original creature and scene, not an adaptation of a named film monster.

**Composition:** a near trunk occupies the left 18% of the frame; another cuts the right edge. A narrow moonlit clearing lies just right of center. Tall interlocking branches form a vaulted canopy across the upper two-thirds. The distant clearing is the readable negative space. The stag's antlers initially participate in that canopy; its head will ultimately sit around 60% frame height, with body extending beyond the lower edge. Sharp near silhouettes, broad middle-distance roots, and a small cool clearing provide three planes.

**Palette/hierarchy:** charcoal and deep neutral green shadows; desaturated mineral-green moonlight; one restrained amber reflection low left. Environment `Y=0.04–0.14`, distant clearing `0.18–0.30`, stag edge `0.25–0.40`, amber fleck `0.50–0.60` over a very small area. No bright eyes. The apparent ribs are glimpsed as missing strips in the background, not white bones filling the scene.

| Time | Image and light action | Intended viewer reading |
|---|---|---|
| 0–4 s | Locked view. Small water reflection breathes under the roots. The clearing gently brightens behind fixed branches; the canopy remains almost still. | Learn where the light and open route are. |
| 4–8 s | The amber fleck drifts left beneath the near trunk. Three thin moonlit branch tips on the right settle a fraction late, while neighboring branches hold. | Something is inconsistent, but not identified. |
| 8–12 s | A broad dark shape crosses the pale opening from right to left. A partial muzzle edge appears for roughly two seconds; the supposed branches rise together as antlers. | Recognize one huge animal embedded in the trees. |
| 12–16 s | The head completes a very slow turn toward the viewpoint. Its antlers pass behind near branches in a stable order. A narrow reflected shape in the water remains facing sideways. Hold the readable pose for the final second. | Main scare: its reflection does not obey its body. |
| 16–20 s | The reflection turns toward the viewer *after* the head has stopped. The real muzzle slips behind the near trunk as the clearing dims. Avoid a flash or lunge. | The viewer's attention transfers to the lower plane. |
| 20–24 s | Creature returns through hidden space behind the trunk. The water settles into its starting phase; the same branch tips and clearing level arrive at the starting state with matching velocities. | Recover atmosphere while concealing the reset. |

**Loop mechanism:** the visible stag trajectory is not reversed in open view. Its pose resets only when fully concealed, while the cyclic environment continues. The lagging reflection fades within a closing ripple before its reset. The initial quiet is the opening phase of the same continuous light cycle.

**Projection risk and fallback:** thin antlers may vanish on textured walls. Simplify into fewer thick silhouette groups, and enlarge the pale gap that defines them. If the water is too low on a badly lit surface, move the reflection cue into a broad luminous band on a central root; preserve the delayed double as the event rather than forcing unreadable detail.

## Treatment B — The Root Choir

**Emotional ambition:** trees become a procession of tall spectral beings whose bodies are never fully visible at once. The forest seems to contain a quiet ritual, and the viewer realizes the procession is coming closer.

**Composition:** three huge split trunks create four vertical viewing slits. A weak amber fissure deep behind the center-right slit acts as the principal source. Woven roots cross the bottom third. The spectral figures are long, folded forms with head cavities and fabric-like surfaces; no smiling skulls, round ghosts or free-floating character outlines. The nearest figure is cropped above and below frame, suggesting scale beyond the available opening.

**Palette/hierarchy:** cold ash and desaturated blue-green surfaces, almost black olive bark, ember-orange source deep in one fissure. Bark `Y=0.04–0.12`; middle planes `0.10–0.18`; backlight `0.30–0.45`; a narrow amber core `0.60–0.65`. One crest of folded material reaches `0.35` during the reveal. Color stays sparse so its later source change has meaning.

| Time | Image and light action | Intended viewer reading |
|---|---|---|
| 0–4 s | Near trunks form an almost abstract cathedral. A slim backlight exposes root depth. A hanging fold at the far-left slit moves once, as if settling. | Establish four separate openings and one continuous space behind them. |
| 4–8 s | The backlight is interrupted sequentially through left, center-left and center-right slits. Each interrupted band has the same notched shoulder contour and progresses at a consistent speed. | One tall form is walking behind several trees. |
| 8–12 s | Two more distant folds travel at slightly different depths. They remain low contrast. The leading form stops in the broadest slit; a hollow head appears without eye lights. | Recognize a procession rather than independent particles. |
| 12–16 s | All distant motion continues, but the leading figure's lower fold lifts from *in front of* a foreground root. The head was apparently far away while a part of the same body is close. Hold 1–2 seconds. | Main scare: depth and scale were misread. |
| 16–20 s | The amber fissure narrows as the nearest figure occludes it. A soft cool edge, motivated by an established high opening, describes the figure's upper turn toward the viewer. The frame never becomes a face closeup. | The procession has acknowledged the spectator. |
| 20–24 s | The near figure folds behind the largest trunk, blocking the problematic depth crossing completely. The amber fissure reopens to its initial width; distant figures exit into concealed root gaps. | Restore the initial spatial puzzle for the next cycle. |

**Loop mechanism:** each figure has its own hidden arrival and departure region. Root/trunk occlusion masks the reset, and all visible folds settle into their phase-matched starting silhouettes. The amber fissure's width and first derivative match at 0/24 seconds. Do not crossfade two whole compositions, which could momentarily make opaque trunks translucent.

**Projection risk and fallback:** four slits might be too busy or narrow. Start with two broad openings in the first physical proof, then add the others only if the procession remains legible from the viewing position. The close-root crossing is the essential event; the distant ensemble is supporting texture.

## Treatment C — The Wing Behind the Moon

**Emotional ambition:** a beautiful lunar opening turns out to be inside the reach of a predatory spectral bird. Its size is revealed by what its wing removes from the sky. The resulting image should feel like a living dark painting, with one impossible anatomical turn.

**Composition:** a veiled moon sits high right, screened by branches; it is a soft luminous patch rather than a glaring white disc. Large near boughs frame a bowl of negative space. Below it is a dark pool with one broken reflection. A massive owl-like form occupies the upper center but initially has the same value as the canopy. Its curved wing eventually spans roughly 70% of the width. The bird has a narrow, irregular face plate and deep head cavity; avoid circular white mascot eyes.

**Palette/hierarchy:** slate blue, pale dead-leaf silver and a trace of tarnished green. No amber accent here, differentiating it from A/B. Near canopy `Y=0–0.06`; distant sky `0.08–0.16`; moon patch `0.35–0.45`; sparse feather edges `0.25–0.35`. At peak reveal a single broad neck plane reaches `0.40`; most of the creature remains a silhouette.

| Time | Image and light action | Intended viewer reading |
|---|---|---|
| 0–4 s | Moonlight filters through a stable branching pattern. A small family of ripples slowly crosses the pool; only one carries the moon's reflection. | A calm, navigable place with one clear light source. |
| 4–8 s | A feather-shaped break appears inside the moon patch and advances slowly. A near branch remains sharply in front of it. No eye cue appears. | Something is passing between the trees and the distant sky. |
| 8–12 s | The interruption expands into a giant articulated wing. Only two overlapping feather groups carry dim edges. The reflected moon disappears fractionally after its sky counterpart. | Recognize huge scale through lost light, not a bright creature sticker. |
| 12–16 s | Wing holds open. A narrow head separates from the canopy and turns; the face cavity is still unlit. At the final moment, its neck silhouette bends through a visibly impossible additional angle while the body remains still. | Main scare: a coherent animal acquires an impossible joint. |
| 16–20 s | The wing closes slowly across the head and almost entirely occludes the moon. During this concealment, a low band of reflected light briefly exposes a second, nearer feather plane at frame bottom. | The creature seems closer than first assumed; attention shifts down. |
| 20–24 s | The wing withdraws behind the highest bough. The moon's veil returns to its original shape, ripples bring back the initial reflection, and the head is reset only while fully hidden. | Calm returns without a bright wipe or conspicuous rewind. |

**Loop mechanism:** the moon veil and water use one 24-second cyclic phase. The creature's visible approach and hidden return are distinct animation segments. Feather-edge lighting must extinguish before the hidden pose reset; it cannot teleport an illuminated outline.

**Projection risk and fallback:** a small bright moon can make the surrounding scene seem blank, and an impossible neck bend can look like a rig error. Keep the source broad and soft, prove the initial natural anatomy first, and hold the later unnatural pose long enough to be intentional. If the creature cannot be recognized without eyes, increase beak/neck/background separation rather than adding two luminous dots.

## Evidence needed before selecting a production treatment

These are proposed review criteria, not completed checks or research findings:

1. Produce a complete moving 24-second animatic of the chosen treatment. Inspect at normal speed and twice consecutively. A contact sheet alone cannot demonstrate the delayed reflection, crossing depth, held recognition or hidden recovery.
2. Check the source's frame-boundary continuity, including optical flow or visible motion at 23.9/0.0 seconds. Examine dark gradients after the actual export/decode path for banding and compression blocks.
3. In the app, confirm that compositing preserves the intended silhouette; test the precomposed scene against a bright underlying layer. Verify actual looping playback, seeking and sustained frame delivery on the target machine. No assumed GPU result should replace this.
4. Project one representative environment, creature reveal and quiet phase onto the real surface at the intended size and ambient light. Record wall material, approximate viewing distance, projector settings and light conditions. Photograph with fixed exposure for comparisons, but also watch the moving result in person because the camera does not establish perceived brightness or fear.
5. Ask viewers what appeared, where it seemed to stand and when they recognized it before asking whether it was scary. A shape that is unidentified or read as a cartoon has failed the scene's premise, even if the rendering is technically smooth. Their emotional response is qualitative feedback, separate from luminance or frame-time measurements.

The strongest first candidate is **The Forest That Holds Its Breath**: it has one large readable creature, an environment-wide transformation and a single measurable temporal relationship between head and reflection. That is an artistic recommendation. Only an actual moving animatic and wall proof can decide whether its restraint reads as dread or simply as dimness.
